# Judging

How projects get to judges, how scores become a ranking, and what we did
about the judge who marks everything a 4. The numbers below come from the
organizer's fixture set and are regenerated by
`python manage.py normalization_report sample-hack-2026`; the full table is
[docs/normalization-proof.md](docs/normalization-proof.md).

## 1. Assignment

Goal: every eligible project gets *k* reviews (the event's
`reviews_per_project`, default 3), judges carry an even load, track
restrictions hold, and nobody reviews their own team.

Algorithm (`judging/services.py::assign_balanced`):

1. Eligible projects = submitted, not hidden, not flagged as duplicates.
2. Count existing assignments per judge (load) and per project, so the run
   can be repeated after adding judges without disturbing what exists.
3. Walk projects from fewest existing reviews to most. For each missing
   review, take the candidate judges who are not already on the project,
   whose tracks include the project's track (or who have no restriction),
   and who are not on the project's team; choose the one with the lightest
   load, breaking ties with a seeded random choice.
4. Record the batch label and write one audit entry with the seed, so the
   organizer can reproduce or explain the run.

Projects that cannot reach *k* (say, a track with two judges and a judge who
is also a team member) are reported back as `short_projects` rather than
silently under-reviewed. Manual assignment and removal exist for the
exceptions; a submitted review cannot be unassigned, only the project hidden.

Judges never see each other's ballots. There is no batch page for judges;
each judge's queue is a query filtered by their own id and their tracks.

## 2. Scoring

The organizer defines the rubric: criteria with names, descriptions and
weights, on a shared integer scale (default 1–5). Weights are relative. A
review is complete when every criterion has a value; drafts can be saved
partially. Each judge may leave one free-text comment per project, exported
alongside the scores.

A judge's score for a project is the weighted mean of their criterion
values on the rubric scale:

```
s(j, p) = Σ_c w_c · v_{j,p,c} / Σ_c w_c        over criteria the judge filled
```

Missing criteria are ignored rather than counted as zero, so a partial draft
does not poison a mean; only *submitted* reviews enter the ranking.

## 3. Why raw averages are not enough

Judges disagree about where "3" is. In the fixture panel, judge means range
from 2.0 to 4.2 and spreads from 0.0 to 0.84. With three reviews per project,
drawing two generous judges is worth roughly half a point on a five-point
scale, which is more than the gap between first and fifth place. Averaging
raw scores therefore ranks luck of the draw along with quality.

## 4. Normalization: per-judge z-scores with shrinkage

Implemented in `judging/normalization.py`, method id `zscore-shrink-v1`.

For each judge *j* with *n_j* submitted reviews, compute their mean *m_j*
and sample standard deviation *σ_j*. For the whole panel compute *M* and *Σ*
over all reviews.

A judge with one or two reviews has statistics that are mostly noise, so
their values are pulled toward the panel with weight

```
w_j = n_j / (n_j + K),   K = 3
m̃_j = w_j · m_j + (1 − w_j) · M
σ̃_j = w_j · σ_j + (1 − w_j) · Σ
```

This is the usual James–Stein-flavoured shrinkage: with one review a judge's
own statistics count 25 %, with three 50 %, with nine 75 %. K = 3 was
chosen because three reviews is where a judge's mean starts to say more
about the judge than about which projects they happened to draw; it is a
constant in the module and documented as such.

Each review becomes a standard score against its judge:

```
z(j, p) = (s(j, p) − m̃_j) / σ̃_j
```

A project's normalized score is the mean of its z-scores mapped back onto
the rubric scale and clipped to it:

```
N(p) = clip( M + mean_j z(j, p) · Σ ,  scale_min, scale_max )
```

Ranking is by *N(p)*, dense (ties share a rank). The raw mean and raw rank
are kept beside it so the organizer can see exactly what moved.

### The flat judge

A judge who gives every project the same score has σ_j = 0 and, more to the
point, has expressed no preference between projects. Dividing by a shrunk
σ̃_j would manufacture a preference out of the panel's spread. Instead their
reviews are given z = 0: they count as "no information" and leave the
project at the panel mean for that review. The judge is flagged `flat` in
the calibration table so the organizer can see it and, if they want, replace
them. In the fixtures this is **jdg_07**, who gave 4/4/4 to all three of
their projects.

### What it does on the fixture data

122 submitted reviews, 40 projects (the duplicate submission is excluded),
30 judges. Panel mean 3.557, spread 0.653.

- **32 of 40 projects change rank.** Most moves are one to three places;
  the top two swap nothing but a tie-break.
- **Largest move: *Small Relay*, raw #12 → normalized #29.** It has two
  reviews: a 4.0 from the flat judge jdg_07, and a 3.33 from jdg_29, for
  whom that is a below-average mark. Half of its raw mean came from a review
  carrying no information; once that is treated as neutral, the informative
  review places it in the lower half. This is the method working as
  intended, not an artefact.
- **jdg_01** has a single review (2.0). With w = 0.25 their mean is shrunk
  from 2.0 to 3.17, so that one harsh mark neither dominates the project nor
  is thrown away.
- **jdg_02**, with six reviews and a mean of 4.22, is a generous judge with
  a healthy spread; their projects move down a place or two relative to raw,
  as they should.

## 5. Community vote

The community tally (sum of vote weights, voided ballots excluded) is stored
next to the judged result as `community_score` and shown as its own column.
It is deliberately **not** blended into the judged ranking: mixing a
popularity count into a calibrated score would let the least controlled
signal move the most controlled one. Organizers who want an audience prize
read the column; organizers who want a blend have both numbers in the CSV.

Quadratic voting is available per event: a voter has *C* credits and putting
*n* votes on one project costs *n²*. It is the most credible published
answer to a loud minority: concentrating influence gets expensive fast, so
broad support beats a small enthusiastic block.

## 6. What we would defend, and what we would change

Defend: per-judge standardization is the standard first step in any panel
scoring problem (it is what conference reviewing systems do), and shrinkage
is the standard fix for small *n*. The flat-judge rule is a conscious
choice with an explanation in the UI, not a division-by-zero guard.

Change with more time: a pairwise mode (Gavel's Crowd-BT) as an alternative
that sidesteps calibration entirely; an interaction term for track
difficulty when judges are track-restricted, since a judge's mean is then
partly the track's mean; and confidence intervals on *N(p)* so the results
page can say when two projects are indistinguishable.
